# What to Look For in an Electric Bike

What to look for in an electric bike comes down to seven things: the class and local rules, the motor and sensor, the battery's size and safety certification, the brakes, the frame fit and weight, the warranty and who services it, and the total price. Get the class and battery right first, because they are the hardest to change later.

I haven't ridden or tested any bike for this page. It is built from NCSL's state-law primer, a Quebec environmental nonprofit's durable e-bike guide ([Equiterre](https://www.equiterre.org/en/resources/311-choisir-un-vae-durable-et-reparable)), a trade-magazine piece on battery standards, and three sellers' buying guides (EVELO, Ampler and Levy). Where those disagree, I show both. Numbers from sellers are their advice, not test results.

## Start with how you'll ride

Before specs, decide the job. EVELO's guide lists city, mountain, road, folding and cargo styles. Levy lists the same, and its advice is to match the type to how you'll ride. A commuter wants fenders, lights and a rack; a hilly route wants more torque; a small apartment may want a folding bike. If you're unsure of the basics, see [what an e-bike is and how it works](/what-is-an-e-bike-how-it-works-and-the-different-e-bike-classes/) and [what a hybrid electric bike is](/what-is-a-hybrid-electric-bike/).

If you will carry the bike upstairs or onto a train, weight matters more than anything else on this list. See [how much an e-bike weighs](/how-much-does-an-electric-bike-weigh-and-what-is-the-lightest-e-bike/) and [the best folding electric bikes](/best-folding-electric-bikes/).

## Class and local rules

What to look for when buying an electric bike starts with the class, because it decides where you can legally ride. [NCSL's primer](https://www.ncsl.org/transportation/state-electric-bicycle-laws-a-legislative-primer) describes the three U.S. classes: Class 1 is pedal assist that stops helping at 20 mph, Class 2 can be propelled by the motor alone (a throttle) with assist stopping at 20 mph, and Class 3 is pedal assist ending at 28 mph and must have a speedometer. The federal definition covers a motor under 750 watts. NCSL counts 26 states using the three-class system, and those states require a label showing the class, top assisted speed and motor wattage, so look for that label.

Helmet and age rules vary by state and class: NCSL lists Connecticut as the strictest, with headgear for all classes, and shows several states with age-based or Class 3 rules. Check yours before buying; my [electric bike laws by state](/pa-california-florida-new-jersey-electric-bike-laws/) page goes further. For how throttle and pedal assist differ, see [do e-bikes need pedaling](/how-fast-do-electric-bikes-go-without-pedaling-or-pedals/).

## Motor and sensor

**Motor position.** Equiterre says mid-drive (pedal) motors are more efficient and balance better than rear hub motors. Levy says hub motors are typically quieter and more straightforward. EVELO recommends a high-torque hub or a mid-drive for hills. Ampler says mid-drives suit mountain or off-road bikes, while most city bikes use hubs. These fit together if you read them as hubs for flat city riding and mid-drives for hills and off-road.

**Power.** What to look for when buying an electric bike for hills is torque more than watts, though none of my sources gives a torque figure. Ampler says around 250 W is "completely sufficient for most applications," which reflects the European 25 km/h assist limit. Levy says more watts mean more power but gives no figures. For U.S. buyers, the federal definition above is the ceiling.

**Sensor.** Equiterre says torque sensors feel more natural and use less battery than cadence sensors, and recommends them. That is one nonprofit's opinion, not a test. Try both on a test ride if you can.

## Battery size, range and safety

**Capacity.** Capacity is measured in watt-hours (Wh). The advice varies:

- **Equiterre:** 400 to 500 Wh is enough for city commuting; choose 625 to 700 Wh for longer distances or hills.
- **EVELO:** 500 Wh or more for long-distance riding; a small battery suits a 10-mile daily commute.
- **Ampler:** more than 300 Wh is enough for most riders, aiming for 50 to 100 km of range.

**Real range.** Equiterre says to plan for 50 to 70% of the advertised range. Heed that: advertised range depends on rider weight, terrain and assist level, as EVELO notes. For the longest-range models, see [the longest-range electric bikes](/longest-range-electric-bikes/).

**Certification.** Equiterre says to look for UL, ULC or CSA marks on both the battery and the charger. A piece in [IAEI Magazine](https://iaeimagazine.org/columns/certification-insights/navigating-lithium-ion-battery-safety-for-e-bikes/) by Intertek's global technical director calls UL 2849 "the ANSI-accredited national safety standard for electrical systems for e-bikes," covering the drive train, battery, charger and wiring, and advises buying only products listed by a nationally recognized testing lab and labeled accordingly. Intertek is a testing company, so that article has its own interest in certification. I'd still check the label myself.

**Care.** Equiterre suggests keeping daily charge between 20 and 80%, and storing at 30 to 60% for winter. See [how to charge an e-bike](/how-to-charge-an-electric-bike-battery-time-cost-and-how-often/). Don't open or repair a battery pack yourself; take it to a shop.

## Brakes, tires and components

Brakes matter more on an e-bike because the bike is heavier and faster. Equiterre prefers hydraulic disc brakes and says mechanical discs cost less but need more adjustment; it doesn't recommend rim brakes. EVELO also recommends hydraulic discs because they "are great for handling the speed." To keep them working, see [how to tighten and adjust e-bike brakes](/how-to-tighten-and-adjust-electric-bike-and-ebike-brakes/).

Equiterre suggests tires at least 1.75 to 2 inches wide for urban commuting, puncture-resistant models, and drivetrain parts from major makers (it names Shimano, SRAM and KMC). It also says obscure brands may leave you with parts that are hard to find later.

## Fit, frame and weight

What to look for in an e bike when it comes to fit: EVELO suggests a step-through frame for comfort and larger frames and wheels for taller riders. Ampler says an upright frame suits the city, smaller frames suit petite riders and a low step-through suits older riders. See [the most comfortable e-bikes for seniors](/most-comfortable-electric-bike-for-seniors/).

On weight, the sellers conflict. EVELO says e-bikes range between 40 and 80 pounds. Ampler says ideally under 20 kg (about 44 pounds, my conversion). These aren't contradictory, since one describes the market and the other a target, but if you lift the bike regularly, weigh it before you buy.

## Warranty, service and price

**Warranty.** The figures differ. EVELO advises at least four years on battery, motor and other parts. Equiterre suggests a lifetime or extended frame warranty, two years on the motor, two years or 500 to 800 charge cycles on the battery, and a transferable warranty for resale. Read the terms; a long headline number can have exclusions.

**Service.** Equiterre says to buy from a local shop with an on-site repair shop, and budgets $150 to $400 a year for mechanical maintenance, excluding the battery. Its page doesn't say which currency, and as a Quebec organization it may mean Canadian dollars.

**Price.** Equiterre's tiers are under $1,500 (wait or buy used, because new bikes may have uncertified batteries and hard-to-find parts), $1,500 to $2,500 for an entry-level bike that should last, $2,500 to $4,000 for regular use and $4,000 to $7,000 for high-end models. Ampler says a good e-bike currently costs around 3,000 euros or more. These are a nonprofit's and a seller's views of what buys quality, not market data, and I haven't checked them against prices today. See [how much an electric bike costs](/how-much-do-electric-bikes-cost-why-so-expensive-and-most-expensive/) and [best budget electric bikes](/best-budget-electric-bikes/).

## A short checklist

1. Class and label match your state's rules.
2. Motor type suits your terrain; sensor type (torque or cadence) tried on a ride.
3. Battery of a size that fits your route, with a recognized certification on battery and charger.
4. Hydraulic or well-adjusted disc brakes.
5. Frame fits you, and you can lift it if you must.
6. Warranty terms read, with a shop that can service it.
7. Total price you can afford, including a helmet and lock.

What to look for in an e bike is mostly this list; the rest is whether the bike feels right when you ride it.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should I look for when buying an electric bike?
Class and local rules, motor and sensor, battery size and certification, brakes, fit and weight, warranty and service, and total price.

### What battery size do I need?
Sources vary: Equiterre says 400 to 500 Wh for city use, EVELO says 500 Wh or more for long rides, and Ampler says over 300 Wh suits most riders.

### Is a mid-drive motor better than a hub motor?
Equiterre says mid-drives are more efficient and balance better. Levy says hubs are quieter and simpler. Mid-drives are favored for hills and off-road.

### How much of the advertised range will I get?
Equiterre says plan for 50 to 70% of the manufacturer's range.

### Do I need hydraulic disc brakes?
Equiterre and EVELO both prefer them. Mechanical discs cost less but need more adjustment.

### What battery certification should I look for?
Equiterre says UL, ULC or CSA on the battery and charger. UL 2849 covers the whole e-bike electrical system.

### How long should the warranty be?
EVELO says at least four years; Equiterre suggests two years on the motor and two years or 500 to 800 cycles on the battery.

## The bottom line

What to look for in an electric bike: the right class for your state, a motor and battery that match your route, certified electrics, good brakes, a fit you can live with and a warranty from a shop that will service it.
