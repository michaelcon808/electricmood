# What Is the Point of an Electric Bike?

What is the point of an electric bike? It is a bicycle with a motor that helps while you pedal, so you can ride farther, handle hills and headwinds, and arrive less worn out. The point is mostly about trips you wouldn't otherwise make by bike. In the largest study I found, people who swapped car trips for an e-bike got a lot more physical activity, while people who swapped regular cycling for an e-bike got slightly less. It is not a free pass on cost, weight or safety, and I cover those too.

I haven't ridden or tested any e-bike for this page. It is built from a seven-city European study, the World Health Organization's activity guidance, Wikipedia's e-bike article (which summarizes other studies), and what four sites (Levy, Ride Review, UCLA Transportation and Ride1Up) say. Where those claims have no data behind them, I say so.

## What is the point of an electric bike? The short version

The sites I read agree on the main idea and give it different weights. Levy says the point of an electric bike is "to offer a versatile, sustainable, and cost-effective mode of transportation." Ride Review says an e-bike lets riders go farther with less exertion, and lists commuting, errands and exploring new terrain. Levy's list adds hills and rough terrain, fitness flexibility, accessibility for different ages and abilities, lower fuel and parking costs, and easier parking.

Those pages give no data for the environmental, congestion or savings claims. That doesn't make them wrong, only unproven there, so the sections below look for something firmer. If you need the basics first, see [what an e-bike is and how it works](/what-is-an-e-bike-how-it-works-and-the-different-e-bike-classes/).

## Longer trips, hills and less effort

This is the core of the point of an electric bike. In the seven-city European survey of over 10,000 people (the PASTA project, published in 2019 and reported on [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/b16178d42c6c4903b5359dd4882e8a03)), e-bike riders averaged 8.0 km of cycling per day against 5.3 km for regular cyclists, and their e-bike trips averaged 9.4 km. The motor didn't make the bike faster so much as make longer rides practical.

Ride Review and Levy both say the motor takes the sting out of hills, and Ride1Up says pedaling is still likely needed on steeper inclines and at higher speeds. If you wonder how much you must pedal, see [do e-bikes need pedaling](/how-fast-do-electric-bikes-go-without-pedaling-or-pedals/). Levy adds that e-bikes let people of different ages and fitness levels ride together without keeping up on pedal power alone. That one is a claim, not a measurement, but it matches why many people buy one.

## Exercise: is an e-bike still a workout?

Yes, but a gentler one per minute, and it can still add up. The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/physical-activity) says adults should get at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity per week.

Here is what the studies in the sources show:

- **The seven-city survey:** e-bike riders had weekly physical activity of 4,463 MET-minutes (about 74.4 MET-hours) against 4,085 (about 68.1) for conventional cyclists, which the authors call similar. A MET is a unit of exercise intensity, and I converted the hours myself.
- **Switching from a car:** travel-related activity rose by about 550 MET-minutes a week (about 9.2 MET-hours, my conversion). Switching from public transport added about 800. Switching from regular cycling cost about 200, a small loss.
- **Intensity:** per [Wikipedia's e-bike article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_bicycle), a University of Tennessee study (Langford, 2017) found e-bike energy use 24% lower than a conventional bike and 64% lower than walking. A cohort study (Dons, 2018) found e-bike users had a higher BMI than conventional cyclists, so the weight-loss claims are anecdotal.
- **UCLA Transportation** says a 2021 study found a 3-mile e-bike commute met the WHO-recommended intensity. Its page gives no sample size, and it says the benefits require pedaling naturally, not just coasting on the motor.

So the point of an electric bike isn't to replace the gym. It's that people actually ride one, and a ride you take beats a car trip you'd have made. The survey is a one-time self-reported comparison, so it shows an association, not proof that e-bikes cause the extra activity.

## Replacing car trips and the environment

The environmental case rests on swapping car trips. The seven-city survey found people who moved from private motor vehicles gained activity, and the authors argue that health benefits from replacing car trips should be considered in e-bike subsidy decisions. I didn't find the share of e-bike trips that replace cars in the pages I read, so I won't give one.

On emissions, Wikipedia reports a 2010 MIT life-cycle study finding e-bikes 18 times more energy efficient than an SUV and 13 times more than a sedan, and a 2011 European Cyclists' Federation estimate of about 15 g CO2e per passenger-km, versus 21 g for an unmotorized bicycle and 50 g for an electric car. It also notes that a beef-heavy diet can push a rider's emissions above 570 g CO2e/km. Those are old figures and the ECF is an advocacy group, so treat them as rough. For the scooter version of this question, see [why electric scooters are good for the environment](/why-are-electric-scooters-good-for-the-environment/).

## Who benefits most

Wikipedia calls the e-bike an "adaptive technology" for weaker, older, disabled or inexperienced riders, and notes cargo models that carry heavy loads or children and electric trikes favored by people with balance disabilities. Levy points to commuters who want to arrive without sweating, and people who want parking to be easy.

Sales suggest it has become mainstream abroad. Wikipedia lists EU sales of over 5 million in 2021 and e-bikes at 57% of bike sales in the Netherlands in 2022. I haven't checked those against the original market reports. For where the market is going, see [electric bike trends and news](/electric-bike-trends-news-urban-market-regulations/).

## The trade-offs: cost, weight, safety and batteries

The point of an electric bike has a price. Wikipedia says an average e-bike in the Netherlands in early 2010 cost three times a regular bicycle, and the battery is a significant share of the cost. See [how much electric bikes cost](/how-much-do-electric-bikes-cost-why-so-expensive-and-most-expensive/) and [how much an electric bike weighs](/how-much-does-an-electric-bike-weigh-and-what-is-the-lightest-e-bike/) for those two.

On safety, Wikipedia reports a U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission estimate of 53,200 e-bike emergency department visits from 2017 to 2022 and 104 e-bike deaths. A California police-report study (2018 to 2024) found e-bike crashes had higher rates of moderate-to-severe injury and unsafe-speed violations than conventional bike crashes. A German study from 2014 found e-bike users were no more likely to be in safety-critical situations, while a 2015 Swedish study found more critical incidents but lower severity. These point different ways, and I haven't resolved them. Wikipedia also notes lithium-ion battery fires have drawn scrutiny since 2019.

Riding doesn't require ignoring any of this: a helmet, good brakes and a battery bought with a recognized safety certification are the sensible basics. For the full list, see [electric bike advantages and disadvantages](/electric-bike-advantages-and-disadvantages/).

## Is an electric bike right for you?

My opinion, not a finding: an e-bike makes most sense if distance, hills or sweat are what keep you from cycling, or if you'd use it to replace some car trips. If you already ride a regular bike happily for short trips, the extra cost and weight may buy you little. If you're not sure, rent or try one before you buy.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the point of an electric bike?
To let you ride farther, handle hills and headwinds and arrive less tired, which makes bike trips practical for more people and more journeys.

### Is an electric bike cheating?
Not by the studies I read: in the seven-city survey, e-bike riders got physical activity similar to regular cyclists because they rode farther.

### Do you still get exercise on an e-bike?
Yes, at a lower intensity per minute. A University of Tennessee study reported on Wikipedia found e-bike energy use 24% lower than a conventional bike.

### Are e-bikes good for the environment?
Sources say yes compared with cars, but the figures I found are old and from advocacy groups, so treat them as rough.

### Who are e-bikes best for?
Commuters, older or less fit riders, people with disabilities, and anyone carrying cargo or kids, according to the sources.

### Are e-bikes dangerous?
Studies disagree. A California study found more severe injuries than on regular bikes, and a German study found no greater risk of critical situations.

### Are e-bikes worth the money?
That depends on how often you would ride instead of drive. Costs are higher than regular bikes, so try one first.

## The bottom line

What is the point of an electric bike? Longer rides, easier hills and more trips by bike instead of by car, at a higher price and weight than a regular bicycle. If that fits how you get around, start with my [best electric bikes](/best-electric-bikes-and-ebikes-for-adults-reviews/) guide.
