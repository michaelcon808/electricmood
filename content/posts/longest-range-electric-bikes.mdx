# Longest Range Electric Bikes 2027

The longest range electric bike 2027 buyers can get, among bikes someone actually tested, is the Aventon Level 4 ADV. Electric Bike Review tested it at 118 miles in Eco mode and 55 miles in Turbo, from an 800 Wh battery, against a maker claim of 110. If you want most of that range for $800 less, the Aventon Level 4 REC tested at 96 to 97 miles in Eco at two sites and costs $1,999. Bikes with two batteries claim 150 to 215 miles, but I found no independent test of those claims.

So what electric bike has the longest range depends on what you count. A tested Eco-mode figure, a tested throttle-only figure and a maker's "up to" number are three different things, and many pages on this topic mix them. I keep them apart and show the watt-hours per mile (my own math) so you can compare a small battery with a big one.

I haven't ridden any of these bikes. Everything here comes from published tests: Electric Bike Review (modified July 28, 2026), Electric Bike Report (July 31, 2026) and OutdoorGearLab (July 9, 2026), plus maker claims where I label them as claims. Where two testers disagree I show both.

## Electric bike longest range 2027: my picks

### Aventon Level 4 ADV: the tested winner

Electric Bike Review lists the Level 4 ADV at 118 miles in Eco and 55 in Turbo, the longest tested range on its page, and above the 110 miles Aventon claims. It costs $2,799 and has a mid-drive Ultro S motor with 750 W peak and 100 Nm of torque, an 800 Wh battery and a 61 lb weight, with classes 1, 2 and 3. At 6.8 Wh per mile in Eco, it is the most efficient of the large-battery bikes in the table. The Turbo number is the one to remember if you ride fast, because 55 miles is the best high-assist result in the tests I found. I haven't ridden it, and the figure comes from one tester.

## What e bike has the longest range when you ride faster?

Most people don't ride in Eco all day, so the high-assist number is the one that sets expectations. In Electric Bike Review's tests, Turbo or the top assist level cut range to roughly 40 to 60 percent of the Eco figure.

| E-bike | Eco or low assist | Turbo or high assist | Eco to Turbo ratio | Wh per mile, high assist |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aventon Level 4 ADV | 118 mi | 55 mi | 2.1 | 14.5 |
| Aventon Level 4 REC | 96 mi | 44 mi | 2.2 | 16.7 |
| Ride1Up Vorsa FT | 87 mi | 40 mi | 2.2 | 18.0 |
| Velotric Discover 3 | 87 mi | 38 mi | 2.3 | 19.2 |
| HeyBike Venus | 83 mi | 32 mi | 2.6 | 21.3 |
| Urtopia Carbon Joy Ultra (529 Wh) | 62 mi | 25 mi | 2.5 | 21.2 |
| Trek FX+1S (520 Wh) | 52 mi | 33 mi | 1.6 | 15.8 |
| Retrospec Chatham Rev+ 2 (500 Wh) | 49.1 mi (Electric Bike Report) | 24.5 mi | 2.0 | 20.4 |

The pattern is that your range at a brisk assist level is roughly half the headline. The Trek FX+1S is the exception, at 52 miles on minimum assist and 33 on maximum (a ratio of 1.6). It has a smaller battery (520 Wh) on a lighter bike, and I don't know why its range falls less at high assist.

Electric Bike Review's Aventure M, tested at about 38 miles in Turbo at 18 to 19 mph, is a reminder that speed matters too: the figure assumes a steady moderate pace, not full throttle. And the Monarc Tracer, which carries two 720 Wh batteries (1,440 Wh in total), was tested at 67 miles in Cruise and 35 in Boost. That is 21.5 and 41.1 Wh per mile on my math, which is far less efficient than the single-battery bikes above, though the mode names differ from Eco and Turbo, so I wouldn't read it as a like-for-like comparison.

## What ebike has the longest range with two batteries?

Dual-battery bikes are where the biggest numbers live, and almost all of them are claims. Here is what I found, with the source and whether it was tested:

| E-bike | Battery | Range figure | Tested or claim | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Velotric GoMad | 801.6 Wh, optional second battery | About 150 mi with second battery | Claim | Electric Bike Review |
| Lectric XPedition 2.0 | 624 Wh to 1,680 Wh options | 60 mi base, up to 170 mi with upgrades | Claim | Electric Bike Review |
| Fiido Titan | 696 Wh, up to 2,088 Wh with three batteries | About 71 mi, up to 215 mi | Claim | Retailer guide (Velorution) |
| Engwe M20 3.0 | 1,200 Wh, dual packs | 90 mi, up to 180 mi | Claim | Retailer guide (Velorution) |
| Monarc Tracer | Two 720 Wh batteries | 67 mi Cruise, 35 mi Boost | Tested | Electric Bike Review |
| Monarc Marker | Two batteries standard | 32 mi on one battery at max assist, windy | Tested | Electric Bike Review |

Two things stand out. First, the claims imply roughly 10 to 13 Wh per mile on my math (1,680 Wh over 170 miles is 9.9, 2,088 Wh over 215 miles is 9.7, and 1,200 Wh over 90 miles is 13.3). That is in line with the tested Eco figures of 5 to 10 Wh per mile, so the claims are plausible only if you ride at low assist on flat ground. Second, the one dual-battery bike with a tested figure, the Monarc Tracer, used about twice that energy per mile.

A simple check: the GoMad's 81 tested miles on 801.6 Wh would double to about 162 miles if a second battery doubled the energy and nothing else changed. The maker claims about 150. That is just arithmetic, not a test, and the extra battery adds weight that costs some range, so I would plan for less. Treat any figure over 120 miles as a best case until a tester measures it.

## Why the testers' range numbers disagree

If you read the three main testers side by side, they look like they are describing different bikes. They are measuring different things.

| Source | How range is measured | Rider and route | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| OutdoorGearLab | Throttle only, bike at its highest power setting, nonstop for up to 3 hours, logged by a Garmin Edge 840 | 180 lb rider, rolling hills on pavement, gravel and dirt | A low-end figure, because the rider doesn't pedal |
| Electric Bike Review | Eco, Turbo and other modes reported separately, with claims marked | 185 to 190 lb riders in the tests that name one, mixed terrain, one route with about 1,350 ft of climbing | The most useful for planning, but no single protocol |
| Electric Bike Report | Says it range-tests each bike, procedure not described on the page I read | Not stated | Useful as a second opinion on the same bike |
| Maker or retailer | "Up to" figure, usually lowest assist | Usually a light rider, flat route | A best case |

OutdoorGearLab's own how-we-test page says that "these bikes can be ridden much farther, with the rider contributing power rather than just pushing the throttle." So its numbers aren't wrong, they are a floor. Here they are with my Wh per mile:

| E-bike | Battery | OutdoorGearLab range | Wh per mile |
|---|---|---|---|
| Segway Xafari | 936 Wh | 38.2 mi | 24.5 |
| Lectric XP4 750 | 840 Wh | 36.7 mi | 22.9 |
| Segway Myon | 722 Wh | 36.1 mi | 20.0 |
| Ride1Up Vorsa ST | 720 Wh | 34.3 mi | 21.0 |
| Aventon Level 4 REC | 733 Wh | 33.4 mi | 21.9 |
| Aventon Aventure 3 | 720 Wh | 28.3 mi | 25.4 |
| Aventon Soltera 3 ADV | 366 Wh | 24.8 mi | 14.8 |
| Lectric XP4 | 500 Wh | 19.1 mi | 26.2 |

Compare the Level 4 REC: 33.4 miles throttle-only at OutdoorGearLab against 96 miles in Eco at Electric Bike Review. That is the same bike, a gap of almost three times, and both can be true. Electric Bike Report also reports the Juiced Scrambler FS (998 Wh) at 43.4 miles on throttle only, which works out to 23.0 Wh per mile and fits the same pattern.

The practical rule: use the Eco figure to see what the bike can do at its best, the Turbo or throttle-only figure to see the worst case, and expect your own ride to land in between. I only found a throttle-only test, an Eco test and a claim for most of these bikes, so I would not trust any single one on its own.

## Small battery, long range: the efficiency side

A big battery is one way to get distance. The other is a bike that uses less energy per mile, and the numbers show it:

- **Velotric Tempo:** 374 Wh, 71 miles in Eco (Electric Bike Review) with a 190 lb rider and about 1,350 ft of climbing. That is 5.3 Wh per mile, the lowest in this guide, and it needs you to pedal.
- **Urtopia Carbon Joy Ultra:** 529 Wh, 62 miles in PAS 1 and about 25 in PAS 5 (Electric Bike Review), 42 lb for the bike.
- **Aventon Soltera 3 ADV:** a 366 Wh battery and a 250 W motor gave 24.8 miles throttle-only at OutdoorGearLab, 14.8 Wh per mile and the best of that table, at 37 lb.

What these have in common is light weight and a modest motor. In the OutdoorGearLab table, the heavy, powerful bikes (Xafari at 94 lb, Aventure 3 at 79 lb) land at about 24 to 25 Wh per mile. A retailer guide (Velorution) says mid-drive motors convert watt-hours into miles more efficiently than hub motors, and the Level 4 ADV, the only mid-drive in my tested table, has the best Eco figure of the big-battery bikes. That is one bike, not proof of a pattern. The Tempo's range depends on your own legs doing real work: a light rider on minimum assist is the case where the maker's number is realistic.

If you value distance per dollar and weight over speed, a 400 to 500 Wh bike at low assist may serve you better than a 1,000 Wh bike you charge less often. For the weight side, see my guide to [the lightest e-bikes](/what-is-the-lightest-e-bike-or-electric-bike-weight/).

## How to estimate your own electric bike range

You can estimate range yourself with one formula: range in miles is roughly battery watt-hours divided by watt-hours per mile. The Wh per mile figures in the tables above are your starting point, using the Eco column if you pedal a lot and the Turbo column if you don't.

| Battery | At 7 Wh per mile (very efficient) | At 10 Wh per mile | At 20 Wh per mile (fast or heavy) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 500 Wh | 71 mi | 50 mi | 25 mi |
| 750 Wh | 107 mi | 75 mi | 38 mi |
| 1,000 Wh | 143 mi | 100 mi | 50 mi |
| 1,500 Wh | 214 mi | 150 mi | 75 mi |

These are my calculations, not tests. They assume you can use the whole battery, and in practice you will lose some at the top and bottom of the charge, so shave them down. They are useful for sanity-checking a claim. If a maker says 150 miles from 750 Wh, that implies 5 Wh per mile, which is lower than anything the testers measured in this guide.

Worked example: you ride a 733 Wh Level 4 REC at a medium assist level between Eco (7.6 Wh per mile) and Turbo (16.7). A midpoint of about 12 Wh per mile would give roughly 60 miles. That is my estimate, and it lands between the two tested numbers, which is the point.

## What cuts e-bike range

The tests give concrete evidence for a handful of factors:

- **Assist level.** The biggest one. The HeyBike Venus went 83 miles at PAS 1 and 32 at PAS 5, the Urtopia Carbon Joy Ultra 62 and about 25. See the table above.
- **Throttle versus pedaling.** OutdoorGearLab's throttle-only test gave 19 to 38 miles on bikes that go 80 to 118 in Eco at Electric Bike Review. Pedaling adds your own power.
- **Speed.** The Aventure M's Turbo figure is quoted at 18 to 19 mph. Electric Bike Review's Urtopia Carbon Atom, with a claim of about 90 miles at lower assist, was tested at 45 miles at max power capped at 20 mph.
- **Terrain.** The GoMad's 81 miles had 1,400+ feet of climbing, the Venus's 83 miles 600 feet. A flat route would likely beat both, but I don't have a flat-route test for those two.
- **Wind.** Electric Bike Review's 32 mile Monarc Marker result at maximum assist on one battery was in windy conditions.
- **Rider weight.** The tests that name a rider use 180 to 190 lb. A lighter rider will likely do better, a heavier one worse, but I haven't seen a test that varies rider weight on the same bike.
- **Tire pressure.** OutdoorGearLab inflates tires to the maximum recommended pressure for its range test. I haven't seen a test that measures how much that changes range.

For cold weather, charging habits and other factors the testers don't measure, see my guides on [riding an e-bike in snow and winter](/can-you-ride-an-ebike-or-electric-bike-in-the-snow-or-winter/) and [how often to charge an e-bike battery](/how-often-should-i-charge-my-e-bike-battery/).

## Big batteries, travel and safety

More range means a bigger battery, and a bigger battery stores more energy. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission counted at least 208 micromobility fire or overheating incidents in 39 states between January 2021 and November 2022, with at least 19 deaths, in a December 2022 [CPSC news release](https://cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2023/CPSC-Calls-on-Manufacturers-to-Comply-with-Safety-Standards-for-Battery-Powered-Products-to-Reduce-the-Risk-of-Injury-and-Death), and asked manufacturers to certify to UL standards. Electric Bike Report notes the Lectric XPedition 2.0 and Aventon Abound LR are UL certified. I couldn't verify certification for the other bikes in this guide, so check the box or the maker's page, charge on a hard surface and don't charge a damaged battery.

Flying with one is a separate question. The FAA's [PackSafe guidance](https://www.faa.gov/hazmat/packsafe/lithium-batteries) limits lithium-ion batteries to 100 Wh each and allows up to two spares of 101 to 160 Wh with airline approval, and its [packing poster](https://www.faa.gov/hazmat/packsafe/resources/spare-lithium-batteries-packing-poster-landscape.pdf) lists 161 Wh and above as forbidden. Every battery in this guide is between 366 Wh and 1,680 Wh by the sources, so none of them fits those limits. The FAA page does not mention e-bikes, so check with the airline or a shipping specialist rather than assuming.

Weight is the other cost of range. The Level 4 ADV is 61 lb, the GoMad 78 lb and the Xafari 94 lb, so a bigger battery bike is harder to lift into a car. Several testers advise removing the battery before lifting.

## Is a long-range electric bike worth it?

It depends on how often you would actually use the range. A few ways to think about it:

- **Daily commuters.** A 20 mile round trip is inside the Turbo range of the larger-battery bikes here (32 to 55 miles). The bigger battery mostly buys you fewer charges, and I don't have a durability test that shows more.
- **Weekend tourers and bikepackers.** This is where 100 mile Eco numbers matter, but remember they assume low assist and a steady pace. Plan on half that at a faster pace.
- **Riders who want a spare.** A dual-battery bike claims much more, but the weight and the lack of tests are real costs.

On price, the cheapest tested way into 90+ mile Eco territory in my sources is the Level 4 REC at $1,999. The ADV adds $800 for another 22 Eco miles, which is a good deal only if you ride in the range where those extra miles matter. If you ride 30 miles a day, they never will. ## Frequently asked questions

### What electric bike has the longest range?
Among tested bikes, the Aventon Level 4 ADV: 118 miles in Eco and 55 in Turbo at Electric Bike Review, from an 800 Wh battery. Dual-battery bikes claim up to 150 to 215 miles, but I found no test of those claims.

### What ebike has the longest range in Turbo?
The Level 4 ADV again, at 55 miles in Turbo, ahead of the Level 4 REC at 44 and the Ride1Up Vorsa FT at 40 (all Electric Bike Review).

### What e bike has the longest range for the money?
The Level 4 REC. It tested 96 to 97 miles in Eco at two sites and costs $1,999. The Level 4 ADV costs $800 more for another 22 Eco miles.

### What is the longest range electric bike 2027 with a throttle?
OutdoorGearLab's throttle-only test put the Segway Xafari first at 38.2 miles from a 936 Wh battery, followed by the Lectric XP4 750 at 36.7 and the Segway Myon at 36.1.

### What is the electric bike longest range 2027 with two batteries?
The claims are 150 miles for the Velotric GoMad with a second battery, 170 for the Lectric XPedition 2.0 with its largest battery, and up to 215 for the Fiido Titan with three batteries. None are independently tested in my sources.

### Do makers' range claims match the tests?
Often the tested Eco figure is higher: the Level 4 ADV was 118 against a 110 claim, the Level 4 REC 96 against 75, the Discover 3 87 against 80. But the Nomad 2X tested 68 miles in Trail mode at PAS 1 against a 75 mile Eco claim, so it is not guaranteed.

### Does a bigger battery always mean longer range?
No. The 374 Wh Velotric Tempo went 71 miles in Eco, while the two-battery, 1,440 Wh Monarc Tracer went 67 in Cruise. Efficiency matters as much as size.

### How far will an e-bike go in Turbo or max assist?
Roughly 40 to 60 percent of its Eco range in these tests: 55 miles for the Level 4 ADV, 44 for the REC, 38 for the Discover 3, and 32 for the HeyBike Venus at PAS 5.

### Why is the OutdoorGearLab range so much lower?
It tests throttle-only at the highest power setting, with a 180 lb rider, for up to 3 hours. The testers say the bikes can go much farther if the rider pedals.

### Can I fly with an electric bike battery?
Probably not. The FAA's PackSafe limits top out at 160 Wh for spare lithium batteries, and every battery in this guide is 366 Wh or more. The FAA page doesn't mention e-bikes, so check with the airline.

### How do I work out my own range?
Divide the battery's watt-hours by your watt-hours per mile. Use about 7 to 10 for gentle Eco riding and 15 to 20 for fast or hilly riding, based on the tested bikes above.

### Is the 118 mile figure realistic?
It is Electric Bike Review's Eco result and it beat Aventon's own 110 mile claim, but it is one tester's number and I don't have the rider weight for it. Plan on about 55 miles at Turbo.

## The bottom line

The longest range electric bike 2027 shoppers can verify is the Aventon Level 4 ADV at 118 miles in Eco, and the best value is the Level 4 REC at 96 to 97 miles for $1,999. Whatever you pick, plan on roughly half the Eco number at a brisk assist level, and treat any two-battery claim over 120 miles as a best case until someone tests it.

Prices and models change, so check the current price and specs on the maker's page before you buy. Since I haven't ridden these e-bikes, treat my picks as a well-sourced shortlist and read a hands-on review of the one you choose.
