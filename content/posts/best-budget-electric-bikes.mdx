# Best Budget Electric Bikes 2027

The best budget electric bike 2027 shoppers can buy with the most independent evidence behind it is the Lectric XP4, which lists at $999 and carries OutdoorGearLab's "Best Under $1000" label. If you need to spend less, the Lectric XP Lite 2 at $799 is the cheapest e-bike that several testers, including Electric Bike Report and Electrek, say they would stand behind. If you can stretch to roughly $1,300 to $1,500, the Ride1Up Roadster V3, the Aventon Soltera line, the Heybike Ranger 3 Pro and the Lectric XP4 750 give you more speed, range or polish. Below about $800 I would not buy: Electrek's reviewer says he has rarely seen anything cheaper that he would trust.

Budget e-bikes give up something, and the test data shows what. In OutdoorGearLab's table the XP4 scored 71 and the XP Lite 2 scored 63, near the bottom of 19 bikes, because their motors struggled on hills and their throttle-only range was short. They are still good value, and this guide explains where each one falls short so you can pick the compromise you can live with.

I haven't ridden these bikes. Everything below comes from OutdoorGearLab, Electric Bike Report, Electric Bike Review, Tom's Guide, Electrek, maker specs and government sources, and I label each number as tested, reported or claimed. Prices are what those sources listed, not live prices. Checked October 9, 2026.

## How I chose these bikes and what budget means

There is no standard price line for "budget". Electrek's price guide splits e-bikes into under $1,000 and under $2,000 tiers at the low end, and Electric Bike Report says its cheap picks "typically start around $1000" with some higher. I treat budget as up to about $1,500, plus a few bikes just above that where the extra money buys a clear step up.

To be on this list a bike had to meet four tests:

- **Independent test data.** At least one tester measured something: range, hill speed, weight or motor output.
- **Certification.** A UL 2849 listing for the system or a documented battery standard, where a source states one.
- **A real company behind it.** A brand with US support, which Electrek's reviewer flags as a main difference between cheap bikes you can rely on and the rest.
- **A price I could source.** No price is used without a named source and a date.

Here is how the testers I rely on measure things, because it changes how you read their numbers:

- **OutdoorGearLab** runs a worst-case range test at the highest power level using the throttle only, with a 180 lb rider and a hilly route, and times a 9% hill. Its range numbers look low next to maker claims on purpose.
- **Electric Bike Report** runs a low-power range test and a max-power range test, plus a timed hill, and reports Wh per mile.
- **Tom's Guide** compares maker range estimates with real rides, tests in rain, wind and at night, and judges the app.

I cannot compare one tester's range number to another's, so I show them side by side and say which is which.


## Best budget electric bike under $1,000

### Lectric XP4: best budget electric bike overall

The XP4 is the folding bike most budget guides name first, and the evidence supports it. OutdoorGearLab lists it at $999, scored it 71, gave it "Best Under $1000" and calls it "an outstanding value for a capable little electric bike." Electrek's guide describes it as "the highest value e-bike I've ever tested". Electric Bike Report's cheap list names it the best cheap multi-purpose bike and says its performance "matched or beat" bikes costing two to three times as much.

What the base model gives you, per the testers: a 500 W hub motor with a 500 Wh battery, a torque sensor, hydraulic brakes, a color display, turn signals, a rack rated for 150 lb and front suspension. It works in Class 1, 2 and 3 modes. Electrek lists a top speed of 28 mph.

What it costs you:

- **Weight.** OutdoorGearLab measured 68.5 lb with the battery, which is heavy for a folding bike to carry upstairs.
- **Hills.** OutdoorGearLab's dyno measured a peak of 909 W and just over 35 Nm of torque, and the bike averaged 13.6 mph up its 9% hill. Its cons list "poor hill performance", a touchy throttle and a somewhat noisy motor.
- **Range.** 19.1 miles on the throttle-only, highest-power, hilly test, against a maker claim of 45 to 85 miles depending on the battery.

OutdoorGearLab says the 750 W version costs about 30% more and adds range, power and accessories, so the base XP4 suits riders who want a basic, low-cost bike. I cover the step up below.

### Lectric XP Lite 2: the lowest price I would trust

At $799 the XP Lite 2 is the cheapest bike in this guide. Electrek's reviewer calls it a rare exception to his rule that bikes under about $800 are not worth trusting, and Electric Bike Report calls it the lowest-priced e-bike it can confidently recommend. OutdoorGearLab says the whole electrical system is UL 2849 certified.

The specs, as reported: a 300 W hub motor (819 W peak by Electrek), a 374 to 375 Wh battery, a single-speed drivetrain, hydraulic disc brakes with 180 mm rotors, lights, a rack and fenders included, and a 20 mph top speed in Class 1 and 2. OutdoorGearLab measured 52 lb. Electric Bike Report measured 47.5 lb. It folds to roughly 36 by 16 by 27 inches.

The tests show what the price means:

- **OutdoorGearLab:** 13.9 miles of throttle-only range on the standard battery and 25.1 miles with the larger one. The 300 W motor overheated on a steep, sustained climb and the tester's bike shut down thermally before the range test finished. It averaged 12.4 mph on its hill test and scored 63, last of the 19 bikes in its table.
- **Electric Bike Report:** 37.2 miles at the lowest assist level (average 9.6 mph) and 19.62 miles at the highest (15.6 mph). On its timed hill the throttle alone averaged 7.6 mph. It also notes "ghost pedaling": with a single-speed drivetrain, pedaling stops affecting speed above about 15 to 16 mph.

Both testers say it fits short, flat trips and small spaces, not hills or long rides. For that use it is the cheapest sensible entry point.

## What you give up with a budget e-bike

Here is where the money goes. Electrek's reviewer lists what he sees on the cheapest bikes: plastic parts, pot-metal crank arms, cheap suspension forks, undersized brake rotors for a 65 lb bike, unbranded motors and controllers that make jerky acceleration, and a lack of long-term frame durability testing. He says a battery marked "48V 10Ah" may deliver only about half that in real use, and that range claims such as 40 miles and "500 W" are often exaggerated. He also notes there is often no support line to call.

You can see some of this in the tests of the bikes above:

- **Motors that are rated higher than they measure.** OutdoorGearLab's dyno found the Roadster V3's "500 W" hub peaked at 638 W on a 250 W motor, and the XP Lite 2's "300 W" motor peaked at 748 W. Peaks are not continuous power, and the Lite 2's motor overheated on a long climb.
- **Heavier bikes at the same price.** The XP4 is 68.5 lb. The Ranger 3 Pro is 76.7 lb. Light bikes like the Soltera 3 ADV (37 lb) cost more or have smaller motors.
- **Basic forks and brakes.** The XP4 750's fork has only 50 mm of travel. The Lite 2 uses a rigid fork.
- **Imprecise battery displays.** Reviewers criticised the Lite 2's vague meter and the XP4 750's 10% battery steps.

My view: for under $1,500, most of these trade-offs are fine. The ones I would not trade are brakes, a certified battery and a company that answers the phone.


## Battery safety, UL certification and the rules

The cheapest bikes are where certification gets skipped, so check it. UL 2849 covers the whole e-bike electrical system, UL 2271 the battery and UL 2272 e-scooters and similar devices. Electrek's reviewer says many ultra-budget e-bikes are not certified to UL 2849 or UL 2271, and that battery fires, which are rare overall, almost always involve non-certified batteries.

The rules are tightening:

- **New York City.** Local Law 39 took effect September 16, 2023. According to the City Council, e-bikes and their batteries must be certified by an accredited lab (UL 2849 for e-bikes, UL 2271 for batteries) before they can be sold, leased or rented in the city, with penalties up to $2,000 per device type, and the city can padlock repeat-offender retailers. See the [City Council announcement](https://council.nyc.gov/press/2024/10/09/2710/).
- **Federal.** In June 2026 the Consumer Product Safety Commission published a [proposed rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/24/2026-12749/safety-standard-for-lithium-ion-batteries-used-in-micromobility-products-and-electrical-systems-of) on lithium-ion batteries in micromobility products. It cites 227 incidents from 2019 through 2023, including 39 deaths and 195 fires, and refers to UL 2849, UL 2272 and UL 2271. It is a proposal, not a final rule, and the comment period closed August 24, 2026. The CPSC has also [called on manufacturers](https://cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2023/CPSC-Calls-on-Manufacturers-to-Comply-with-Safety-Standards-for-Battery-Powered-Products-to-Reduce-the-Risk-of-Injury-and-Death) to comply with the safety standards for battery-powered products.

From the testers I used: OutdoorGearLab says the XP Lite 2's system is UL 2849 certified. Electric Bike Report lists UL 2849 for the Ranger 3 Pro (with a UL 2271 battery) and for the Soltera 2.5 via TÜV Rheinland, and lists the XP4 750 as UL 2849 and 2271 (the page's formatting is garbled, so confirm on the maker's page). The Roadster V3 page I read lists UL certification as "N/A", so I would ask before buying it where that matters.

Charging habits matter too: the federal notice says 53% of the incidents it counts happened while the product was plugged in. For the cost side, see my guide to [what it costs to charge an e-bike](/how-much-does-it-cost-to-charge-an-ebike-or-electric-bike/).

## Budget electric bike buying checklist

Before you pay, check these six things. My longer [buying checklist](/what-to-look-for-when-buying-an-electric-bike-or-e-bike/) goes further.

1. **Certification.** Look for UL 2849 for the system and UL 2271 for the battery on the maker's page, not only in the listing.
2. **Brand and support.** Can you reach a person by phone or chat in the US, and what does the warranty cover?
3. **Brakes.** Hydraulic discs with 180 mm rotors are what the Lite 2 and Portola ship with. Mechanical brakes on a heavy bike are a reason to look elsewhere.
4. **Weight.** If you carry the bike up stairs, check the measured weight, not the claim. Several bikes here weighed more than their claims.
5. **Battery size.** Compare Wh, not volts or amp-hours alone. A 360 Wh battery is a commuter's battery, and a 700 Wh battery is a long-day battery.
6. **Your hills.** If you have steep climbs, avoid the Lite 2 and be cautious with the base XP4; step up to the XP4 750 or a stronger motor.

The Lectric XP Lite 2 at $799. Electrek and Electric Bike Report both say it is the lowest-priced e-bike they would recommend, and OutdoorGearLab lists it as UL 2849 certified. Below about $800, Electrek's reviewer says he has rarely seen anything he would trust.

### Are cheap electric bikes any good?
Some are. The ones from established brands with certified batteries and support can be good value, but they give up hill power, range or weight. Bikes under about $800 from unknown sellers often use generic parts, according to Electrek.

### Is a $1,000 electric bike good enough for commuting?
For flat commutes, yes. The XP4 and Portola weigh about 58 to 71 lb, which matters on stairs, and the Roadster V3 at about $1,300 to $1,500 is a lighter commuter.

### What is the best budget electric bike under $1,500?
The Ride1Up Roadster V3 for a light commuter, the Heybike Ranger 3 Pro for comfort, and the Lectric XP4 750 at $1,299 for range and hills.

### How far can a budget e-bike go on a charge?
In OutdoorGearLab's worst-case tests, between about 14 and 27 miles, versus maker claims of 25 to 85 miles. At low assist, Electric Bike Report measured 37 to 63 miles on some bikes.

### Do budget e-bikes have UL certification?
Some do, many ultra-cheap ones do not. OutdoorGearLab lists the XP Lite 2 as UL 2849 certified, and Electric Bike Report lists UL 2849 for the Ranger 3 Pro and Soltera 2.5. Check before buying.

### Is it better to buy a budget e-bike or convert a bike?
A conversion kit can cost less if you already own a good frame, but I haven't compared them with these bikes. See my conversion kit guide for options.

### Which budget electric bike is best for hills?
Among the bikes here, the Lectric XP4 750. Electric Bike Report called it the fastest folding bike it had timed on a hill, while the XP Lite 2 overheated on a steep climb at OutdoorGearLab.

### Which budget electric bike is lightest?
The Aventon Soltera 3 ADV at 37 lb (OutdoorGearLab), then the Ride1Up Roadster V3 at about 39 to 44 lb depending on the tester, and the Lite 2 at about 48 to 52 lb.

### Can I use a budget e-bike on bike paths?
Rules vary by place. Class 3 bikes (28 mph pedal assist) usually face more limits than Class 1 and 2, so check local rules first. My guide to the [e-bike classes](/what-are-the-different-e-bike-classes-class-1-2-3-and-4/) explains the differences.

## The bottom line

If you want the best budget electric bike 2027 offers with the strongest evidence, buy the Lectric XP4 at $999 and accept its weight and modest hill power. If you have less to spend, the XP Lite 2 at $799 is the lowest price I would trust, for flat, short rides. If you can reach about $1,300 to $1,500, the Lectric XP4 750 gives the best range and hill performance in this guide, the Roadster V3 is the light commuter, and the Ranger 3 Pro is the comfortable one. Whatever you choose, look for UL certification, hydraulic brakes and a company that answers its phone.

Prices and models change, so check the current price and specs on the maker's page before you buy. Since I haven't ridden these e-bikes, treat my picks as a well-sourced shortlist and read a hands-on review of the one you choose.
