# Electric Bike Pros and Cons

Electric bike pros and cons come down to this: you get farther, handle hills with less effort, and in the largest study I found riders got about as much exercise as regular cyclists, but you pay more, carry more weight, face a battery that wears out in roughly 3 to 5 years, and take on injury and battery-fire risks that the safety data does not let me call small. Those electric bike advantages and disadvantages are the ones the sources agree on, and I show where they don't.

I haven't ridden or tested any e-bike for this page. It draws on the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC), a seven-city European study, Wikipedia, HealthDay's report on CPSC injury data, Electric Bike Report, DrivingElectric, a Quebec nonprofit's buying guide (Equiterre), and battery and buying guides from EVELO, Ampler, HOVSCO and Aventon, which are sellers. Where a figure is a seller's estimate, I say so.

## The pros of an electric bike

**Longer rides and easier hills.** In the seven-city European survey of over 10,000 people (the PASTA project, reported on [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/b16178d42c6c4903b5359dd4882e8a03)), e-bike riders cycled 8.0 km a day on average against 5.3 km for regular cyclists. EVELO, a seller, says 10 miles on a regular bike can become "closer to 20 miles" with an e-bike, a claim without a source.

**Exercise.** The same survey found e-bike riders had weekly activity of 4,463 MET-minutes against 4,085 for regular cyclists, which the authors call similar, because e-bike riders rode farther. People who moved from a car gained activity. That survey is a self-reported snapshot, so it shows an association. Wikipedia adds the other side: a University of Tennessee study (Langford, 2017) found e-bike energy expenditure 24% lower than on a conventional bike.

**Replacing car trips.** The survey's authors argue that health benefits from replacing car trips should count in e-bike subsidy decisions. I found no figure for what share of e-bike trips replace a car.

**Low running costs.** EVELO says recharging costs far less than transit fares or gas and that there are no licensing, registration or parking costs, and DrivingElectric says servicing needs are no greater than a regular bike's. Neither gives figures. For real charging costs, see [how to charge an e-bike](/how-to-charge-an-electric-bike-battery-time-cost-and-how-often/) and [how much e-bikes cost](/how-much-do-electric-bikes-cost-why-so-expensive-and-most-expensive/).

**Energy efficiency.** Electric Bike Report says a 2019 Italian study showed e-bikes use 40 times less energy than a gas car over the same distance. I couldn't read that study. Wikipedia reports a 2010 MIT life-cycle study finding e-bikes 18 times more energy efficient than an SUV and 13 times more than a sedan. The two comparisons use different vehicles, so I don't treat them as a single number.

**Access.** Wikipedia calls the e-bike an "adaptive technology" for weaker, older, disabled or inexperienced riders. Electric Bike Report says less fit riders can keep up with friends and family, based on the author's own bike shop anecdotes. See [what to look for in an electric bike](/what-to-look-for-when-buying-an-electric-bike-or-e-bike/) for choosing fit and comfort.

## The cons: price and weight

**Price.** EVELO says e-bikes typically cost $1,000 to $10,000, which it calls a significant upfront investment. DrivingElectric says they are "heavier and more expensive than a regular bike." For why prices vary, see [how much an electric bike costs](/how-much-do-electric-bikes-cost-why-so-expensive-and-most-expensive/), and for lower-priced options, [best budget electric bikes](/best-budget-electric-bikes/).

**Weight.** Electric Bike Report says most e-bikes weigh upwards of 45 lb, making them hard to lift, carry upstairs or put on a rack, and that heavier bikes may need e-bike-rated racks and tip over more easily. That statement has no source. EVELO says only that they are "noticeably heavier." If you must lift your bike, check the weight before buying: see [how much an e-bike weighs](/how-much-does-an-electric-bike-weigh-and-what-is-the-lightest-e-bike/) and [best folding electric bikes](/best-folding-electric-bikes/). DrivingElectric notes you can still pedal when the battery is flat, though you are pedaling the extra weight.

## The cons: battery wear and replacement cost

Three sources agree on the lifespan and differ on the price.

- **Lifespan.** Ampler, HOVSCO and Aventon all say 3 to 5 years, or roughly 500 to 1,000 full charge cycles. Ampler says lithium-ion batteries keep about 80% of capacity after 500 full cycles. HOVSCO adds that heavy daily commuting may cut life to 2 to 3 years, and casual riding with good storage can pass 5. These are makers' and sellers' figures, not independent tests.
- **Replacement cost.** Ampler says €300 to €1,000. HOVSCO says $300 to $1,200 (£250 to £900+), with proprietary systems such as Bosch, Shimano and Yamaha at $800 to $1,200+ and recelling at $200 to $500. Aventon gives one pack price, $499 for a Pace 500.2 battery, and says its integrated battery is serviced by an authorized center, with the owner paying labor.
- **Warranty.** HOVSCO says 2 years or 500 cycles is common. Aventon's warranty is 2 years or 300 cycles, whichever comes first, with up to 75% capacity retained.

So the con is real but bounded: budget for a battery replacement within 3 to 5 years, and expect to pay several hundred dollars. How you charge matters; Ampler and Aventon both advise keeping the battery between about 20% and 80% when you can. See [how to charge an e-bike](/how-to-charge-an-electric-bike-battery-time-cost-and-how-often/).

## The cons: injuries and battery fires

This is where the sources disagree most, so I show the numbers and the wording.

**Injuries.** [HealthDay's report](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/public-health/e-bikes-2665990912.html) on the CPSC's October 17, 2023 data says micromobility injuries rose nearly 21% from 2021 to 2022, with about 46% of estimated e-bike injuries in 2022 alone, and that 233 deaths were associated with these devices from 2017 to 2022, though reporting is incomplete. [Wikipedia's e-bike article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_bicycle) reports a CPSC estimate of 53,200 e-bike emergency department visits from 2017 to 2022 and 104 e-bike deaths, 45% of micromobility deaths. It also reports a California police-report study (2018 to 2024) in which e-bike crashes had higher rates of moderate-to-severe injury and unsafe-speed violations than regular bike crashes, and notes a 2014 German study that found e-bike users no more likely to be in safety-critical situations, while a 2015 Swedish study found more critical incidents but lower severity. I haven't read the CPSC report itself.

**Fires.** The [CPSC's release](https://cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2023/CPSC-Calls-on-Manufacturers-to-Comply-with-Safety-Standards-for-Battery-Powered-Products-to-Reduce-the-Risk-of-Injury-and-Death) (dated December 20, 2022 on the page, though its address says 2023) counted at least 208 micromobility fire or overheating incidents in 39 states between January 1, 2021 and November 28, 2022, with at least 19 deaths, 3 of them involving e-bikes, and at least 22 injuries treated in emergency departments, 10 involving e-bikes. The products covered include scooters, hoverboards, e-bikes and e-unicycles. The CPSC asked more than 2,000 manufacturers and importers to certify products to UL 2272 (personal e-mobility devices) or UL 2849 (e-bikes).

Compare the tone elsewhere: DrivingElectric calls battery or motor fires "exceedingly rare," usually from tampering, and Electric Bike Report calls thermal runaway "very uncommon," usually with damaged batteries. Neither gives a rate, and the CPSC counts are incident counts without a denominator, so I can't turn any of this into a risk per rider. My reading: the risk is not large for any one rider, but it is real, and the practical steps are to buy a bike whose battery and charger carry a recognized certification and to use the charger that came with it.

**Learning curve.** Electric Bike Report says e-bikes are heavier and accelerate faster than regular bikes, and an accidentally pressed throttle when mounting is a hazard. That is the author's view, with no data.

## The cons: upkeep, rules and everyday hassle

**Maintenance.** The sources split. DrivingElectric says servicing is no more than a regular bike's, roughly once a year in the U.K. Equiterre, a Quebec nonprofit, budgets $150 to $400 a year for mechanical maintenance, excluding the battery, in its buying guide and recommends a local shop. Which is closer depends on your bike and how much you ride. Readers on Electric Bike Report raised parts availability and warranty difficulties outside the original seller, which are comments, not findings.

**Rules.** Class, speed limits and where you can ride vary by state and country; see [what an e-bike is and the classes](/what-is-an-e-bike-how-it-works-and-the-different-e-bike-classes/) and [electric bike laws by state](/pa-california-florida-new-jersey-electric-bike-laws/). Electric Bike Report also lists grey areas in the rules and some etiquette friction, including riders who call e-bikes cheating.

**Travel.** DrivingElectric says airlines limit battery size in hand and checked luggage for fire-safety reasons, so taking an e-bike abroad takes planning.

**Theft.** Electric Bike Report's commenters raise theft and locks that can be defeated. I found no theft statistics, so I can't size it.

## Who should buy one, and who should skip

My opinion, built on the sources above:

- **Buy** if distance, hills or sweat keep you from riding, or if you would swap regular car trips for it. The survey and the access argument fit you.
- **Think twice** if you must carry the bike up stairs, have no secure place to store or charge it, or ride rarely. The weight, price and battery wear cost you most.
- **Skip** if you already ride a regular bike happily for short trips and are not limited by distance or hills. The extra cost may buy you little.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the main electric bike pros and cons?
Pros: longer rides, easier hills, exercise similar to regular cycling and low running costs. Cons: higher price, extra weight, battery replacement every 3 to 5 years, and injury and battery-fire risks.

### What are the biggest electric bike advantages and disadvantages?
The biggest advantage is that more trips become practical by bike. The biggest disadvantages are price, weight and the battery's cost and wear.

### Do e-bikes give you exercise?
In the seven-city survey, e-bike riders had weekly activity similar to regular cyclists because they rode farther. Intensity per minute is lower.

### How long does an e-bike battery last?
Ampler, HOVSCO and Aventon all say 3 to 5 years, or roughly 500 to 1,000 full charge cycles.

### How much does it cost to replace an e-bike battery?
Ampler says €300 to €1,000 and HOVSCO says $300 to $1,200. These are seller estimates.

### Are e-bikes dangerous?
The CPSC reports rising injuries and counted 3 e-bike deaths among 19 micromobility fire deaths from 2021 to November 2022. Whether per-rider risk is high isn't answered by these counts.

### Are e-bike battery fires common?
Two sources call them rare, and the CPSC counted 208 micromobility fire or overheating incidents in 2021 and 2022. None gives a rate per rider.

### Are e-bikes heavy?
Electric Bike Report says most weigh upwards of 45 lb.

## The bottom line

Electric bike pros and cons, in short: longer rides and hills made easy, similar exercise, cheap to run, against a high price, extra weight, a battery that wears out in 3 to 5 years and real injury and fire risks.
