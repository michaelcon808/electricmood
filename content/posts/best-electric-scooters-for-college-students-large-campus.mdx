# Best Electric Scooters for College Students 2027

The best electric scooter for most college students is the NIU KQi Air: ERideHero measured 24.2 miles of range from it, it weighs only 26.4 lb, and it has an IP55 water rating. If you want to spend less, the Hiboy S2 is the budget pick at 31.9 lb with tires that never go flat. If your campus is big or you commute from off campus, the NAVEE ST3 Pro gives you real distance and folds small enough for a trunk, though at 55.8 lb you won't want to carry it far.

Those are my picks for the best electric scooters for college students 2027 buyers should look at. The scooter is the easy part, though. The bigger risks for a student are campus rules that ban riding on sidewalks, rules that ban charging or storing the battery in your dorm, and a lock that fails. This guide covers all three, because a scooter you can't legally ride or charge on your campus is a bad purchase at any price.

I'll be straight about how this was built. I haven't ridden these scooters. My picks come from published tests by ERideHero and Electric Scooter Guide, and campus rules come from the universities' own pages, which I link below. I say where a number is a maker's claim. Most competing student guides I found were short brand posts that mostly recommend their own scooters, so I've tried to make this the independent version. I'm writing in October 2026 and I'll update this page as 2027 models arrive.

## Check your campus rules before you buy

This is the step most buying guides skip, and it can decide which scooter you can own. Campus policies differ a lot, and several changed recently. Here is what I found on university pages. I link each one, and you should read your own school's version, because these were current when I checked and policies get revised.

| School | What the rule says |
|---|---|
| [Miami University (Ohio)](https://codes.ohio.gov/assets/laws/administrative-code/authenticated/3339/0/16/3339-16-19_20260516.pdf) | Ohio Administrative Code 3339-16-19, effective May 16, 2026. E-scooters may only be ridden in campus bike lanes, and are barred from sidewalks on university property. The limit is 15 mph on campus roads and 8 mph when sharing space with pedestrians. Parking lots and garages are no-ride zones. Riders can't carry passengers, wear headphones or carry packages. Devices and their batteries may not be present, stored or charged in residence halls, other buildings or anywhere on university property. Violations can bring a $25 citation, impoundment with a $75 fee, or both |
| [University at Buffalo](https://www.buffalo.edu/ubnow/briefs/2024/11/e-scooters.html) | Published November 2024. E-scooters are prohibited on campus sidewalks, green space and inside university buildings. Off campus, don't ride on public sidewalks, use bike lanes or the road edge, yield to pedestrians and don't exceed 15 mph |
| [University of Alabama](https://news.ua.edu/2026/08/ua-rolls-out-new-campus-policy-for-bikes-scooters/) | New policy announced August 2026 covers devices up to 30 mph and requires them to be registered with parking services. Devices and batteries are never allowed inside buildings, including residence halls. The campus has 15 covered park-and-charge locations with 282 charging spaces |
| [Western Carolina University, Policy 29](https://www.wcu.edu/discover/leadership/office-of-the-chancellor/legal-counsel-office/university-policies/numerical-index/university-policy-29.aspx) | Effective August 1, 2026. Devices under 750 watts must be registered or they can be impounded. Charging from any campus outlet, inside or outside, is prohibited |
| [Texas A&M, Residence Life](https://reslife.tamu.edu/rules/personal-electric-vehicles/) | Devices that meet registration requirements may be stored in a student's residence with roommate consent. Chargers must draw 4 amps or less, third-party chargers aren't allowed and charging must stop at full. Devices must be walked through buildings |
| [Prairie View A&M](https://www.pvamu.edu/bsrv/scooter/) | Personal electric vehicles are prohibited in buildings, charging inside buildings is prohibited, and a motor over 750 watts or 1 HP is prohibited on campus |
| [UC Davis](https://transportation.ucdavis.edu/devices-indoors) | Charging inside publicly accessible university facilities is prohibited, and charging in residential units is limited to UL-certified devices, or an EN equivalent |

Some of these pages cover e-skateboards by name and describe the rules for personal electric devices in general, so read the scooter wording on your school's page. What the rows show is the range you might face: from "allowed with registration and a charging cap" to "no devices or batteries anywhere on university property". Four things to check before buying:

1. **Is riding allowed, and where?** Bike lanes only, roads only, or nowhere on campus.
2. **Is there a speed or power cap?** Examples above are 15 mph, 30 mph and 750 watts. The power rule is ambiguous in some policies, because they don't say whether the cap is per motor or total. Ask.
3. **Do you have to register?** Alabama and Western Carolina both do.
4. **Can you store and charge it in your room?** This is the big one, and it has its own section next.

## Dorm charging and storage: the part that decides your scooter

Lithium-ion batteries are a fire concern, and universities have responded. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission counted at least 208 micromobility fire or overheating incidents in 39 states between January 2021 and November 2022, with at least 19 deaths, 5 of them involving e-scooters, in a December 2022 [CPSC news release](https://cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2023/CPSC-Calls-on-Manufacturers-to-Comply-with-Safety-Standards-for-Battery-Powered-Products-to-Reduce-the-Risk-of-Injury-and-Death). That release also asked manufacturers to certify products to the voluntary UL standards, including UL 2272 for personal e-mobility devices. Schools read the same numbers, which is why the rules above are so strict.

Here is what that means in practice:

- **A removable battery doesn't solve it.** One competing guide implies you can carry a swappable battery to your room. At Miami University, devices and batteries can't be present or charged in any building, and at Alabama batteries are never allowed inside. A swappable battery like the TurboAnt X7 Max's helps only where the policy allows it.
- **Ask your housing office in writing** if you plan to keep the scooter in your room. Get the answer before you buy.
- **Look for designated charging.** Alabama's 15 park-and-charge locations are an example of a school providing it.
- **Where charging is allowed, follow the terms.** Texas A&M's example is a charger rated 4 amps or less, no third-party chargers and unplugging at full. UC Davis limits residential charging to UL-certified devices.
- **If your school bans it all, have a plan.** An off-campus apartment, a friend's garage or a commuter-lot locker may be the only option. If there's no workable plan, a different mode may suit you better, such as a [skateboard for campus commuting](/best-electric-skateboard-for-college-campus-commuting/), which hits the same battery rules, or a bike.

I couldn't verify the certification status of each scooter below, so check UL 2272 on the maker's page. UC Davis's rule shows why it matters.

## How I chose these scooters

I filtered for what a student actually deals with, not for the highest spec sheet.

1. **Weight.** Levy's student guide says under about 30 lb is ideal for carrying a scooter to class and up dorm stairs. That's a brand blog, but it matches what I'd expect, and ERideHero's lightweight list keeps all six of its picks between 26 and 39 lb for the same reason.
2. **Tested range, not claimed range.** ERideHero says claimed range is often 40 to 60 percent higher than what it measures. Its lightweight page adds that an extra 100 lb of rider weight can cut range by 20 to 30 percent, which matters if you carry a backpack.
3. **Enough range for a campus, not more.** Levy says most campuses are small enough that 10 to 20 miles per charge is usually enough. I agree for a normal campus, and I cover big campuses separately below.
4. **Water resistance.** You'll ride in rain at some point.
5. **Parts and support.** Every pick is from an established brand.
6. **No prices.** They change weekly, and the sources I read disagree.

I left out seated scooters, which are hard to store in a dorm, and heavy performance scooters, which are overkill for campus and often banned by speed or power caps.

## Best electric scooters for college students large campus 2027

"Large campus" is the problem that range numbers actually solve, so here's how to size it. Instead of guessing, count your day.

1. **Count your crossings.** A large campus can mean 1 to 2 miles between a dorm, lectures, the library and food. Eight crossings of 1.5 miles is 12 miles.
2. **Add the trip home, or the off-campus commute.**
3. **Add a buffer of about a third,** because you might not be able to charge during the day, and batteries lose range as they age.
4. **Convert miles to watt-hours.** In my guide to [long-range scooters](/best-affordable-top-long-range-electric-scooters-for-adults-longest-range-reviews/), I calculated from tested data that commuter-class scooters use roughly 19 to 27 watt-hours per mile, so multiplying by 24 is a sensible planning number. That's my own estimate from different testers' data.

On that math, 12 miles of crossings plus a one-third buffer is 16 miles, which is about 385 Wh. The KQi Air at 451 Wh and the ST3 Pro at 597 Wh clear it. The Hiboy S2 at 270 Wh does not, and it would need a mid-day top-up that your campus may not allow. That's the reason the best electric scooters for college students large campus 2027 shortlists lean toward bigger batteries, even though weight goes up with them.

If your campus is big and your policy bans charging, the heavy end of the range doesn't help, because you'll be unable to refill it. In that case, favor a lighter scooter you can carry to a place that allows charging.

Count the stairs in your life: dorm, lecture halls, buses. ERideHero's lightweight page says to count the stairs on your route and check which transit you'll use. ## Locking it up and preventing theft

Campus bike racks are where scooters disappear. Levy's student guide advises folding the scooter and bringing it indoors rather than leaving it at a rack, using a heavy-duty U-lock through the frame and a wheel if it must stay outside, and never leaving it out overnight. That's a brand blog, but it's the same advice I'd give.

A few notes of my own:

- **Check whether your campus lets you bring it into buildings at all.** At Miami and Alabama it doesn't, so you can't carry it into a lecture hall, and the lock is your only protection. At Texas A&M you may need to walk it through buildings.
- **Tracking helps.** Electric Scooter Guide notes that the NAVEE ST3 Pro supports Apple Find My, and GOTRAX's student guide says its G5 has a passcode lock. Those are deterrents and tracking aids, not substitutes for a good lock.
- **Register it if your school offers registration,** which makes recovery easier.

For a step-by-step approach, see my guide on [how to lock up an electric scooter to a bike rack](/how-to-lock-up-an-electric-scooter-to-a-bike-rack/).

## Weather and water resistance

A campus rider gets rained on. The picks above range from IPX4 (Hiboy S2, TurboAnt X7 Max) to IP55 (NIU KQi Air) and IPX5 (Segway E3 Pro, NAVEE ST3 Pro). Those ratings describe resistance to splashes and water jets in a lab test, not a promise that the scooter survives a downpour. Water damage is often excluded from warranties.

For a full explanation of the ratings and what to do after a wet ride, see my guide to [riding an electric scooter in the rain](/can-you-ride-use-or-drive-an-electric-scooter-in-the-rain/). Wet pavement also affects stopping, so slow down.

## Safety and riding rules on and off campus

A few rules from the campus pages above are worth treating as habits wherever you ride:

- **Wear a helmet.** Buffalo's guidance includes it explicitly.
- **Yield to pedestrians** and keep to 15 mph or less where posted. Miami drops the limit to 8 mph when sharing space with pedestrians.
- **Use bike lanes or the road edge,** not sidewalks, off campus too, where the law or your school says so.
- **Skip headphones.** Miami prohibits them.
- **Don't carry passengers.** Miami prohibits that too.
- **Check whether a backpack counts as carrying a package** under your school's rule, if it has one.

If you've never ridden before, read my [beginner's guide to riding an electric scooter](/how-to-ride-an-electric-scooter-for-beginners-first-time/) and practice somewhere quiet. State and city laws also vary, and I didn't summarize them here, so check your local rules.

## Cost, budget and whether a scooter is worth it

I haven't quoted prices because they change and the sources disagree. For example, one tester listed the Segway Ninebot E2 Plus II at $349 and another at $449. What I can tell you is how to think about cost:

- **The scooter is only part of it.** Add a lock, a helmet and possibly a spare battery. For a full breakdown, see my guide on [how much an electric scooter costs](/how-much-does-an-electric-scooter-cost-to-buy/).
- **Light and cheap means short range.** The Hiboy S2's 11 tested miles is the price of being light and affordable.
- **Weigh it against alternatives.** If you'd otherwise pay for parking permits or rideshares, a scooter can pay off, but only if you can legally ride it and store it. A [budget electric bike](/best-budget-electric-bikes/) is another route if your campus is bike-friendly.
- **Shop the sales,** such as back-to-school and holiday promotions, but compare against the maker's listed specs, not a retailer's.
