# Can You Bring a Skateboard on a Plane? Electric Boards Too

Yes, you can bring a skateboard on a plane. In the US, the TSA allows skateboards in carry-on and checked bags, and your airline decides the size and weight limits. An electric skateboard is a different story: the battery, not the board, decides whether it can fly, and the key number is its watt-hours.

So can you bring a skateboard on an airplane? A regular one, almost always. An electric one, only if the battery fits the airline's lithium rules and you've checked with the airline first. That's the whole answer, and the rest of this guide is the detail that stops you getting turned away at the gate.

I'm writing this the way I'd explain it to a friend packing for a trip. I haven't flown with every board and I won't pretend otherwise. The rules below come from the TSA and the FAA, and I link them where I use them. Airlines can be stricter than the government, so treat your airline's own policy as the final word. ## What the TSA says about skateboards

Start with security, because that's the part people worry about. According to the [TSA's "What Can I Bring?" list](https://tsa.gov/travel/security-screening/whatcanibring/all?page=39), skateboards are allowed in carry-on bags and in checked bags, and the TSA asks you to check with your airline for any size or weight restrictions. The same page adds that the final decision rests with the TSA officer at the checkpoint.

Two things follow from that. First, a skateboard isn't a prohibited item, so you can stop worrying that it'll be confiscated at the scanner. Second, the TSA isn't the one who decides whether it fits in the cabin. That's your airline, and the rules differ from one carrier to the next.

If you've ever asked "can I take a skateboard on a plane" and got a different answer from every forum, that's why. Security says yes. So can you bring a skateboard on an airplane in the US? Yes. Whether your particular board rides in the cabin or the hold depends on its size and on the airline.

## How to bring a skateboard on a plane: carry-on or checked?

The practical question is where the board goes. Here's how I'd decide.

1. **Check the airline's carry-on size and weight limits.** A short cruiser or street deck often fits in the overhead bin or under a seat. A longer deck may not.
2. **Measure your board with the wheels on.** Airlines measure the whole item, not just the deck.
3. **If it fits, carry it on.** Your board stays with you, and you're not trusting baggage handling with your trucks and deck.
4. **If it doesn't fit, check it.** Pack it well, and ask about any fee for sports equipment.
5. **Be ready to gate-check.** If the cabin is full, a gate agent may ask to tag your board for the hold.

That's the short version of how to bring a skateboard on a plane. A few packing habits make either route smoother, and these are my own suggestions, not rules:

- Put the board in a padded skate bag or wrap it well, with the wheels covered so they can't scuff other luggage.
- Keep it flat and secure, since loose trucks can snag on straps and tags.
- Take a photo of the board and the packed bag before you check it, in case you need to make a damage claim.
- Put any tools in your checked bag if you can, so there's nothing to explain at security.
- Remove or cover anything sharp or heavy, and don't rely on the board's weight to protect the bag.

## Can you bring skateboards or a longboard on a plane?

Can you bring skateboards or a longboard on a plane? Skateboards are skateboards as far as the TSA is concerned, so a longboard isn't banned either. The catch is size. A longboard is often longer than the airline's carry-on limit, which usually means it goes in the hold.

If you're travelling with more than one board, think about it like any other sports gear:

- **Each board takes space.** Two boards in one bag add up in weight and length, so check the airline's allowance for the bag, not just the board.
- **Fees can apply.** Some airlines charge for sports equipment or for an extra checked item.
- **Padding matters more for long decks.** Longboards flex, and a hard knock can crack a deck or bend a truck.

My advice is to look up your airline's sports-equipment page before you buy a bag, because the size limits for a checked item decide which bag you need.

## Electric skateboards: the battery is what matters

Everything above applies to a regular board. An electric skateboard adds a lithium-ion battery, and aviation rules treat those batteries carefully because lithium-ion cells can overheat.

The [FAA's PackSafe guidance on lithium batteries](https://www.faa.gov/hazmat/packsafe/lithium-batteries) sets the thresholds that matter for you:

- Spare (uninstalled) lithium-ion batteries must be carried in carry-on baggage only. If a carry-on bag is checked at the gate, spare batteries must be taken out and kept with you in the cabin.
- Lithium-ion batteries are limited to a rating of 100 watt-hours (Wh) per battery.
- With airline approval, you can carry up to two spare larger lithium-ion batteries of 101 to 160 Wh.
- The FAA's [packing poster](https://www.faa.gov/hazmat/packsafe/resources/spare-lithium-batteries-packing-poster-landscape.pdf) lists batteries of 161 Wh and above as forbidden, and notes that airlines may have stricter limits.

Put another way, the battery fits one of three categories, and it decides how your electric skateboard travels.

| Battery rating | What the FAA says for a spare battery | What it means for your electric skateboard |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 100 Wh | Allowed in carry-on | Usually the simplest case. Carry the battery in the cabin |
| 101 to 160 Wh | Allowed only with airline approval, maximum two, carry-on only | Ask your airline before you travel and get the approval in writing |
| 161 Wh and above | Forbidden | The battery can't fly as a passenger item |

One honest limit on what I can tell you. The FAA text I read is written mainly around spare batteries. A board with the battery built in, or one that can't be removed, falls into a grey area that depends on the airline. So if your board's battery is larger than 100 Wh, or you can't take it out, don't guess. Ask the airline how it treats that exact board.

## How to work out your battery's watt-hours

You don't need to guess your battery's rating. The FAA tells you how to work it out: multiply the battery's voltage by its amp-hours. A battery labelled 36 V and 5 Ah is 180 Wh. One labelled 36 V and 2.5 Ah is 90 Wh.

Where to look:

1. **The battery label.** Many newer batteries print the Wh rating directly. The FAA notes that newer lithium-ion batteries often have it marked on them.
2. **The spec sheet or the manual.** Look for "Wh", or voltage and capacity (V and Ah or mAh).
3. **The manufacturer's support page.** If it's not on the label, the maker can tell you.
4. **The battery itself, for removable packs.** Take a photo of the label and keep it on your phone. If a security officer or gate agent asks, you can show it.

If the number isn't printed anywhere, don't fly with the battery until you've confirmed it. An unmarked battery is a bigger problem at the gate than a marked one.

## How to pack an electric skateboard for a flight

If your board's battery is within the limits, here's how I'd pack it. These steps follow the FAA's rule that batteries must be protected from damage, plus the short-circuit protection [CASA describes](https://www.casa.gov.au/node/422727), which means taping over exposed terminals or using a separate pouch, and a bit of common sense.

1. **Take the battery out, if the board allows it.** Spare batteries go in carry-on only.
2. **Protect the terminals.** Tape over any exposed contacts or use the original packaging, and put each battery in its own pouch or bag so nothing can bridge the terminals.
3. **Keep the battery with you in the cabin.** Don't put it in checked baggage.
4. **Power the board off completely.** Don't just sleep it. If it has a power switch, make sure it's off, and turn the remote off too.
5. **Pack the board so it can't turn on.** Wedge or tape the remote so the controls can't be pressed.
6. **Carry the charger separately**, and keep the Wh rating label and spec sheet easy to reach.
7. **Check the board for damage first.** A swollen, dented or leaking battery shouldn't travel at all. Stop using it and get it looked at by the maker.

If the battery isn't removable, the board needs the airline's say-so before you go, and I'd get that in writing.

## What to ask your airline before you fly

Your airline's policy is the final word, and they publish it. Before you fly, check the website and, for anything unusual, contact them. These are the questions I'd ask:

- Is an electric skateboard allowed at all, in the cabin and in the hold?
- What's the maximum battery size, in Wh, that you allow?
- Does a battery between 101 and 160 Wh need approval, and how do I get it?
- Can the battery stay installed, or does it have to come out?
- Does the board count as sports equipment, and what are the size and fee rules?
- If I'm connecting with another airline, does their policy match yours?

Ask for the answer by email or in the chat transcript, and screenshot it. If a gate agent disagrees, you've got something to show them. It won't guarantee boarding, but it helps.

## Flying outside the US

Lithium battery limits aren't a US-only idea. Australia's aviation regulator, CASA, publishes the same pattern for spare batteries: [spare lithium-ion batteries above 100 Wh and up to 160 Wh need airline approval, with no more than two spares, and can be carried on but not checked](https://www.casa.gov.au/node/422727). Other countries have their own security and customs rules on top, and airlines apply the stricter of the two.

For an international trip, I'd do three things: check the rules in your departure country, the arrival country and every connection; check your airline's lithium policy rather than relying on the headline limit; and keep your Wh label and spec sheet handy for customs and security.

## Mistakes that get boards stopped at the airport

These are the ones I'd watch out for:

- **Assuming the TSA's yes covers the battery.** A skateboard is fine at security. A battery is covered by a different set of rules.
- **Putting a spare battery in checked luggage.** The FAA says spare batteries go in carry-on only.
- **Not knowing the Wh rating.** If you can't say what it is, you'll lose time at the gate.
- **Flying with a damaged battery.** Swelling, dents, heat or a burning smell mean it stays home.
- **Skipping the airline's own page.** The airline can be stricter than the FAA.
- **Leaving the remote on.** A board that can power up in a bag is a problem.
- **Forgetting the connecting flight.** Different carriers have different rules.

A short checklist you can screenshot:

1. Confirm the airline allows the board and the battery.
2. Find the Wh rating (volts times amp-hours) and photograph the label.
3. Get approval in writing if the battery is above 100 Wh.
4. Take the battery out, protect the terminals and carry it on.
5. Power the board off and turn the remote off.
6. Check the board's size against the carry-on and checked limits.
7. Pad the board and photograph it before you check it.

If you're buying a board partly for travel, check the battery's Wh rating before you buy. It's the single detail that decides whether a board can fly. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can you bring a skateboard on an airplane?
Yes. The TSA allows skateboards in carry-on and checked bags, so you can bring a skateboard on an airplane. Your airline sets the size and weight limits.

### Can I take a skateboard on a plane in my carry-on?
If it's within the airline's carry-on size and weight limits, yes. If not, it has to be checked, or gate-checked if the cabin is full.

### How to bring a skateboard on a plane without damaging it?
Pad it in a skate bag, cover the wheels, keep it flat, and photograph it before checking it. Carry it on if it fits.

### Can you bring skateboards or a longboard on a plane?
Yes, but longboards are often too long for the cabin, so expect to check them. Two boards add to your bag's weight and may add fees.

### Can you take an electric skateboard on a plane?
Possibly. It depends on the battery's watt-hours, whether it's removable and what your airline allows. Up to 100 Wh is the simplest case.

### How many watt-hours can you take on a plane?
The FAA limits lithium-ion batteries to 100 Wh each, with up to two spare batteries of 101 to 160 Wh with airline approval. Batteries of 161 Wh and above are forbidden.

### Does the battery have to be in carry-on?
Spare lithium-ion batteries must be in carry-on only, according to the FAA. If your bag is checked at the gate, the batteries must come out and stay with you.

### Can I check an electric skateboard?
Check the airline's policy first. Spare batteries can't go in checked bags, so the battery generally has to be removed and carried on.

### How do I find my battery's watt-hours?
Multiply the volts by the amp-hours, or look for the Wh rating on the label or spec sheet.

### What if my battery is above 160 Wh?
The FAA lists 161 Wh and above as forbidden, so it can't travel as a passenger item.

### Can I bring the charger?
Check your airline's policy. I'd pack it in your carry-on with the battery paperwork, separate from the battery itself.

### Will the TSA confiscate my skateboard?
Skateboards aren't prohibited, but the TSA says the final call is the officer's. Be polite and have your documents ready.

## The bottom line

Can you bring a skateboard on a plane? Yes, a regular one is allowed in carry-on and checked bags in the US, and your airline sets the size limits. An electric board comes down to the battery: know the Wh rating, carry the battery in the cabin, get approval in writing if it's between 101 and 160 Wh, and don't fly with anything at 161 Wh or above.

Check the airline's policy before you pack, because they can be stricter than the FAA. If you're still choosing a board, read the battery specs first, then start with [the best electric skateboards](/best-electric-skateboards-reviews/).
