# What Is an E-board? Electric Skateboards Explained 2027

What is an e board? It is an electric skateboard: a skateboard with a motor, a rechargeable battery and a speed controller, usually steered with a wireless hand remote. The motor does the pushing, so you go faster and farther with less effort than on a regular board. "E-board" is shorthand, and the same thing also goes by electric skateboard and e-skateboard.

This guide explains what is inside one, how it works, the main types, and what a first-time rider should know. If you already know the basics and want speed figures, see [how fast electric skateboards go](/how-fast-do-electric-skateboards-go/).

I haven't ridden or tested these boards. What follows comes from Wikipedia and from the beginner guides published by makers Maxfind and Meepo, and I say where they disagree or where something is my own opinion.

## What is an e-board made of?

If you are asking what is an e board in practical terms, it is six parts working together. Wikipedia and the two maker guides agree on most of them:

| Part | What it does |
|---|---|
| Deck | The board you stand on. Most are multi-layer wood (Maxfind names maple or bamboo for flex, and carbon or fibreglass for stiffness at higher speeds). Wikipedia says length, wheelbase, flexibility and width all affect stability |
| Motor | Turns the wheels. Hub motors sit inside the wheel; belt-drive motors sit under the deck and spin the wheel with a toothed belt |
| Battery | A lithium-ion pack, usually under the deck. Wikipedia says batteries often take several hours to charge fully |
| Electronic speed controller (ESC) | Turns your remote's throttle input into motor power, handles braking and watches temperature (Maxfind) |
| Remote | A hand-held wireless throttle. Maxfind says it talks to the board over 2.4 GHz or Bluetooth |
| Wheels and trucks | Wheels are 90 to 120 mm per Maxfind. Wikipedia puts the line between street and all-terrain wheels at 115 mm. Trucks are the metal assemblies that hold the wheels under the deck |

The battery also has a battery management system (BMS), which Maxfind says balances the cells and cuts power before the pack is over-discharged.

## How does an e-board work?

You stand on the deck and hold the remote. Pushing the thumb wheel or trigger forward tells the ESC to send more power to the motors. Wikipedia adds that on some boards you can also shift weight toward the front to accelerate and toward the rear to brake, and that you steer by tilting the board left or right.

**Braking.** Most e-boards use regenerative braking. When you ease off or pull back on the throttle, the ESC reverses the current, which slows the board and feeds some energy back into the battery (Maxfind). Wikipedia says this braking acts on the rear wheels only, and that stopping distance varies widely between motors and tires. In my view that is the most important thing for a new rider to understand: a board speeds up with more confidence than it stops.

**Speed modes.** Meepo recommends starting in Beginner Mode and avoiding Pro Mode at first, and says new riders may need about a month of practice before feeling comfortable at top speed. That is the maker's advice, not a tested figure.

## Hub motor or belt drive: which e-board is which?

The two motor types you will see most often behave differently:

| | Hub motor | Belt drive |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Inside the wheel | Under the deck, linked to the wheel by a belt |
| Noise | Quiet (Maxfind, Wikipedia) | Louder, which Wikipedia says can make the board obvious in cities |
| Upkeep | Nearly maintenance-free (Maxfind) | Needs occasional belt tensioning (Maxfind) and belt replacement (Wikipedia) |
| Power delivery | Less torque (Wikipedia) | Punchier acceleration and braking for the same power (Maxfind, Wikipedia) |
| Wheels | Limited choice (Wikipedia) | Easy to swap (Maxfind) |

Wikipedia also lists direct drive (a motor between the wheel and truck) and gear drive. It says dual motor is the most popular setup, with single-motor boards typical of entry level and four-motor boards also made.

## Types of e-board: mini, street, all-terrain and more

Wikipedia groups boards like this:

- **Mini.** Short, light boards, usually under 11 kg, built for portability and short trips, often with a detachable battery. Wikipedia notes some detachable packs are under 99 Wh, which it calls air-travel safe. For flying rules see [can you bring a skateboard on a plane](/can-you-bring-or-take-a-skateboard-on-a-plane-or-airplane/), and for models see [best mini electric skateboards](/best-mini-electric-skateboards/).
- **Street (flexy).** Light boards with street wheels and a flexible deck.
- **All-terrain (2-in-1).** Wheels of 115 mm and up, usually belt-drive dual motors, for city riding plus grass, gravel and light off-roading.
- **Off-road.** Built on mountain-board decks with at least dual motors and optional suspension, for rough terrain and steep hills. See [best off-road electric skateboards](/best-off-road-all-terrain-electric-skateboards/).
- **Racing.** Low, stiff decks and wide rubber wheels. Wikipedia says some controllers output over 15 kW at peak.
- **Folding.** Designed to travel easily.

Meepo also splits boards by deck length: shortboards at 30 inches and under, and longboards over 30 inches, and it advises beginners to pick a longboard deck for stability. Wikipedia agrees that high speed almost always needs a longboard.

## What do people call an e-board?

Is an e-board the same as an electric skateboard? Yes. Ride Review lists the names "electric skateboard" and "e-skateboard," and defines it as a skateboard with an electric motor and battery. Maxfind's beginner guide mostly says electric skateboard and uses "e-board" once, in its discussion of city speed caps on bike paths. So when shops and review sites use any of these terms, they mean the same product. I haven't found a standard that defines "e-board" as a separate category.

## A short history of the electric skateboard

Per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_skateboard):

- **1975.** The gasoline-powered MotoBoard was released.
- **1997.** California banned motorized skateboards, aimed at gas-powered ones because of noise and pollution. That year Louie Finkle of Seal Beach offered a wireless electric skateboard, and he filed a patent in April 1999.
- **2004 to 2006.** Motors and batteries became efficient enough to power boards effectively.
- **2012 to 2013.** The ZBoard raised nearly 30 times its Kickstarter target and drew attention at CES in January 2013.
- **2017.** California's law was changed by Governor Jerry Brown.

Wikipedia says the next generation of boards, the Boost Board era, were lighter, used electric remotes, and had higher speeds and longer battery life.

## How fast and how far does an e-board go?

Wikipedia says typical retail boards reach around 20 to 25 mph in their fastest modes. Maxfind says beginner boards top out near 20 mph. The full breakdown, with tested and claimed figures, is in [how fast do electric skateboards go](/how-fast-do-electric-skateboards-go/).

Range is where the two Maxfind guides differ. One says beginner boards go 8 to 12 miles on a full charge. The other says boards typically last 10 to 30 miles per charge and recommends 10 to 20 miles for most beginners. I'm showing both because they are different claims from the same maker, and rider weight, cold and hills all cut range.

## What a beginner should know before buying an e-board

- **Gear.** Maxfind calls protective gear non-negotiable for beginners: a helmet, knee and elbow pads and wrist guards. Wikipedia notes several fatal accidents and says 2018 to 2022 injury data found more hospitalizations and multiple fractures per accident on electric skateboards than on traditional ones.
- **Practise first.** Meepo advises learning foot pushing and foot braking before using the motor, starting in an empty parking lot, and moving to bike paths only when you can start, stop and turn. Maxfind recommends beginners ride around 10 to 15 mph.
- **Rules.** Laws differ by place. Both Maxfind and Wikipedia say some places treat e-boards like bikes and others restrict sidewalks or bike lanes. Check your local rules.
- **Price.** Maxfind says entry-level boards typically cost $400 to $700. Meepo says its boards range from under $300 to under $1,000. Treat both as maker statements, not market averages.
- **Wet weather.** I cover it in [can you skateboard in the rain](/can-you-skateboard-in-the-rain-electric-skateboard/).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is an e board in simple terms?
A skateboard with a motor and battery that you control with a hand-held wireless remote.

### What is an e-board called in stores?
Usually an electric skateboard or e-skateboard. Ride Review lists both names.

### What is an e board remote?
A wireless hand-held throttle. Maxfind says it sends acceleration and braking commands over 2.4 GHz or Bluetooth.

### What is an e board motor?
Either a hub motor inside the wheel or a belt-drive motor under the deck, per Maxfind and Wikipedia. Wikipedia also lists direct drive and gear drive.

### Are e-boards hard to ride?
They take practice. Meepo says new riders may need about a month before feeling comfortable at top speed.

### How do e-boards brake?
Mostly by regenerative braking on the rear wheels, controlled from the remote (Maxfind, Wikipedia).

### Is an e-board the same as a Onewheel?
No. They are different rides. See my Onewheel guide linked above.

### Can you take an e-board on a plane?
It depends on the battery. Wikipedia says some detachable packs under 99 Wh are air-travel safe. Check the airline's rules.

### Do you need a licence for an e-board?
Rules vary by place. Wikipedia says electric skateboards are typically unregistered and uninsured, and that regulation is debated. Check local law.

## The bottom line

So, what is an e board? A motorised skateboard you ride with a hand remote, built from a deck, motor, battery, controller and wheels. The main choices are hub or belt drive and mini, street, all-terrain or off-road, and the main skill is learning to stop as well as you go.
